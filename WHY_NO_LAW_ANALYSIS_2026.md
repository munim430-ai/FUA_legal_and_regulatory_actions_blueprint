# Why There Is No Law: The Political Economy of Bangladesh's Education Consultancy Black Hole

**Prepared:** June 2026  
**Type:** Analytical Report — Political Economy + Regulatory Failure  
**Scope:** Bangladesh overseas education consultancy sector, 2000–2026

---

## The Core Question

Over **90,000 Bangladeshi students per year** pay fees to unregulated education consultancies. At a conservative average of BDT 50,000 per student, that is **BDT 450 crore (~USD 41 million) in consultancy fees alone — every year** — flowing through a sector with zero mandatory licensing, zero government audits, and near-zero tax compliance. Add tuition remittance routed through hundi networks and the real figure is in the thousands of crores annually.

Yet no dedicated law exists. No ministry claims ownership. No enforcement happens.

**This is not an oversight. It is a system.**

---

## Part 1: The Numbers — What Is Actually At Stake

### 1.1 Scale of the Market

| Metric | Figure | Source |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Bangladeshi students abroad per year (2023) | **52,799** (official) | UNESCO / Ministry of Education |
| Students consulting agencies annually (estimated) | **70,000 – 90,000** | Financial Express BD |
| Conservative agency fee per student | **BDT 30,000 – 3,00,000** | Industry reports |
| Registered consultancy members (FACD-CAB) | **~400** | FACD-CAB |
| Estimated actual consultancies operating | **~2,000+** | Financial Express BD |
| EU visa rejections of Bangladeshi applicants (2024) | **30,000+** | EU border data |
| Growth in outbound students (2008→2023) | **3× increase** (16,000→53,000) | Ministry of Education |

**Market revenue floor calculation:**
- 70,000 students × BDT 50,000 average consultancy fee = **BDT 350 crore/year minimum**
- Top-end (BDT 2 lakh per student × 20% of volume) = additional **BDT 280 crore**
- Language course fees (Korean, IELTS, etc.) = **BDT 100–200 crore** additional
- **Estimated total annual sector revenue: BDT 600–1,000 crore (USD 55–90 million)**

### 1.2 Tax Revenue Lost

VAT at 15% on declared services, plus income tax, plus BIN registration fees:
- If the sector were fully formalised and taxed: **BDT 90–150 crore/year in VAT alone**
- In 15 years of explosive growth (2010–2025): estimated **BDT 1,350–2,250 crore in unpaid VAT**
- Income tax on profits: additional hundreds of crores

### 1.3 The Hundi Pipeline

Every student who remits Korean university tuition via hundi (not through formal banking) contributes to:
- **Illegal forex outflow** — Bangladesh Bank loses foreign currency that should stay in reserves
- **Hundi operator profits** — 1–2% commission on billions in transfers
- **Tax-free money movement** — no audit trail, no capital gains tax

Bangladesh lost an average of **USD 16 billion annually in illicit financial outflows** between 2009 and 2023. A white paper commissioned by the interim government confirmed **USD 234 billion was siphoned during the Awami League's 15-year rule** — an "unholy alliance" of politicians, businessmen, and financial actors who "corrupted the country's executive, legislative, financial, legal and other institutions." The education consultancy sector's hundi flows are a small but structurally identical piece of this same ecosystem.

## Part 2: The Seven Real Reasons Nothing Has Been Done

### Reason 1: Jurisdictional No-Man's Land — Nobody Owns It

Education consultancies in Bangladesh fall between **five different ministries**, none of which claims full jurisdiction:

| Ministry | Partial Claim | Why It Doesn't Act |
|----------|--------------|-------------------|
| Ministry of Education | Overseas admissions → universities | Focused on domestic; views outbound as "not our students anymore" |
| Ministry of Expatriates' Welfare | Overseas work/migration | BOESL/BMET only covers labour migration, not education |
| Ministry of Commerce | Trade in services | Considers education consultancy a "business service," not its mandate |
| Ministry of Home Affairs | Immigration, visa fraud | Reactive (criminal enforcement only); no regulatory role |
| Ministry of Finance (NBR) | Tax registration | Only acts when revenue is explicitly reported/denied; not proactive |

**Result:** Each ministry gestures at another. In bureaucratic terms, this is called "buck-passing" — a feature, not a bug, for industries that benefit from unregulation.

The University Grants Commission (UGC) oversees domestic private universities but has **no statutory mandate over overseas recruitment**. Bangladesh Bank's BFIU has mandate over financial crimes but does not register businesses. DNCRP has mandate over consumer protection but does not issue licences.

> **There is no single agency whose budget, headcount, or KPIs are tied to regulating education consultancies. Therefore, none acts.**

---

### Reason 2: The Informal Economy Is Bangladesh's Real Economy

Bangladesh's **tax-to-GDP ratio was 6.6% in FY2024–25** — the lowest in Asia, marginally above conflict economies like Yemen and Sudan. The informal economy constitutes **30–40% of GDP**. Out of 170+ million people, **fewer than 2.5 million filed income tax returns** in 2021–22.

This is not administrative incompetence. It is the equilibrium of a society where:
- The **political class** funds itself through informal business networks, not formal taxation
- The **business class** treats tax compliance as optional and enforcement as negotiable
- The **NBR** lacks the headcount, digital infrastructure, and political backing to pursue enforcement
- **CPD research** found tax evasion in 2023 alone amounted to **BDT 2.26 trillion (Tk 2,26,000 crore)** — education consultancy VAT evasion is a rounding error on that number

> Education consultancy tax evasion is invisible inside Bangladesh's broader tax non-compliance catastrophe. When you cannot collect taxes from major corporations, garment factories, and landowners, why would you send inspectors after a small consultancy in Mirpur?

---

### Reason 3: Political-Business Nexus — The System Protects Its Own

The same structural feature that allowed **USD 234 billion to leave Bangladesh through illicit channels** enables education consultancy fraud to operate freely. The white paper on Bangladesh's economy (2024) described a system where:

> *"Corrupt politicians, businessmen, and financial players worked in connivance and corrupted the country's executive, legislative, financial, legal and other institutions... through an unholy alliance."*

This systemic capture operates at multiple levels in the education sector:

**Level 1 — Local political patronage:** Many consultancy owners are active in local Awami League (previously) or BNP networks. They hold ward-level positions, contribute to election campaigns, and receive protection from local police and magistrates in return.

**Level 2 — Trade association capture:** FACD-CAB (Foreign Admission and Career Development Consultant Association of Bangladesh) was the industry's self-regulating body. Trade associations in Bangladesh historically function as **lobbying vehicles** for existing operators — their incentive is to keep entry barriers low for themselves while blocking the kind of formal licensing that would invite government scrutiny.

**Level 3 — Commission from foreign universities:** Legitimate consultancies receive **10–15% commission** from foreign universities (Korean, Australian, Canadian, UK) for each enrolled student. This creates a multi-billion taka flow of undeclared foreign income that operators have every incentive to keep off government books. A formal licensing regime would require disclosure.

**Level 4 — Hundi network connections:** The informal remittance networks that move student tuition money to Korea, Australia, and Canada are run by operators with **deep roots in Bangladesh's informal financial system** — people who are connected to powerful figures in banking, politics, and business. Regulating education consultancies would disrupt the hundi pipelines that serve many powerful interests simultaneously.

---

### Reason 4: International Pressure Goes in the Wrong Direction

When Australia doubled student visa fees, Canada restricted Bangladeshi applications, and the EU rejected 30,000+ applications in 2024, the **pressure landed on Bangladeshi students and legitimate applicants** — not on the fraudulent consultancies that caused the problem.

Bangladesh's response to international scrutiny has been:
- **Blame the students** for submitting fraudulent applications (rather than the consultancies that prepared them)
- **No domestic prosecution** of consultancies whose false applications caused visa rejection waves
- **No public blacklist** of fraudulent consultancies (unlike Australia's CRICOS deregistration system)
- **No financial penalty on consultancies** for visa rejection rates

> The perverse outcome: legitimate students suffer visa rejection while fraudulent operators continue taking fees from the next round of applicants.

---

### Reason 5: Regulatory Design Failure — The Gap Between Labour and Education Migration

Bangladesh has a **reasonably functional** labour migration regulatory system through BMET (Bureau of Manpower, Employment and Training) and BOESL, with mandatory agency licensing under the Emigration Ordinance 1982. This covers workers going to the Middle East, Malaysia, and Southeast Asia.

But when the Emigration Ordinance 1982 was written, **student migration did not exist at scale.** In 2000, fewer than 5,000 Bangladeshis studied abroad. By 2023, it was 53,000. The law simply never caught up.

**The legislative gap is structural:**
- Emigration Ordinance 1982: covers "workers," not "students"
- Private University Act 2010: covers domestic universities, not overseas recruitment
- Consumer Rights Protection Act 2009: provides remedy after fraud, not prevention
- VAT Act 2012: captures registered businesses, not informal operators

Every relevant law was written for a different purpose. None was designed to capture an industry that emerged a decade after the laws were passed.

---

### Reason 6: The Enforcement Capacity Simply Does Not Exist

Even if a law were passed tomorrow requiring education consultancy licensing, Bangladesh lacks the institutional infrastructure to enforce it:

| Enforcement Gap | Reality |
|----------------|---------|
| **NBR field officers** | ~4,000 VAT inspectors covering 165,000+ sq km and ~8 lakh registered BINs — no capacity for new sector |
| **DNCRP offices** | 64 district offices with minimal staff; handles millions of consumer complaints across all sectors |
| **CID Cyber Crime Unit** | Focused on major cybercrime; cannot monitor thousands of consultancies' Facebook ads |
| **Ministry of Education** | No field inspection capacity for private businesses |
| **Digital records** | Most consultancy transactions are cash or mobile banking (bKash/Nagad) — hard to audit |

> CPD research found NBR has roughly 1 tax officer per 47,000 people — compared to India's 1 per 5,000. **You cannot enforce what you cannot audit.**

---

### Reason 7: The Industry's Customers Cannot Organise Politically

The victims of education consultancy fraud are **structurally powerless**:

- They are **young, first-time consumers** of a complex service they do not understand
- Many are **from outside Dhaka** — less able to navigate regulatory complaint systems
- They paid fees in **cash or mobile banking** — difficult to prove
- After going abroad, **they cannot return to complain** without losing their visa status
- Those who were defrauded and stayed in Bangladesh are **socially reluctant to publicise failure** due to family expectations
- **No organised victim group** has formed with sufficient political leverage to force legislative change

Compare this to, say, garment workers (organised unions) or traders (powerful chambers of commerce) — groups that have successfully blocked or shaped legislation affecting them. Student victims have no equivalent political infrastructure.

## Part 3: The Money — Where Does It Actually Go?

### 3.1 The Fee Chain

```
Student pays BDT 2,00,000 to consultancy
        ↓
~50%: Retained as consultancy profit (undeclared, untaxed)
~20%: Paid to sub-agents, middle-men (undeclared)
~15%: Application/visa processing costs (some legitimate)
~15%: "Partnership fees" to foreign institution (often via hundi)
        ↓
Zero VAT paid. Zero income tax. Zero NBR record.
```

### 3.2 The Tuition Remittance Chain

```
Student needs to pay Korean university tuition: ~USD 3,000-8,000/semester
        ↓
Bangladesh Bank allows USD 10,000/year for student remittance (formal)
        ↓
REALITY: Most agencies route via hundi to avoid documentation requirements
        ↓
Hundi operator in Dhaka collects BDT equivalent
Hundi operator's partner in Seoul/Busan releases KRW equivalent
        ↓
Zero FERA compliance. Zero Bangladesh Bank record. Hundi operator earns 1-2%.
```

**Estimated tuition remittance via hundi (annual):**
- 30,000 active overseas students × USD 5,000 average annual tuition = **USD 150 million**
- If 50% goes via hundi (conservative): **USD 75 million/year in illegal forex outflow**
- Over 10 years: **USD 750 million in education-related hundi alone**

This is why Bangladesh Bank periodically warns about "digital hundi" targeting the study-abroad market — but issues no enforcement, because the hundi operators are too integrated into the financial system to touch without collateral damage.

## Part 4: What Changed in August 2024 — And What Didn't

On **5 August 2024**, Sheikh Hasina's government fell following a mass student uprising. The interim government of **Muhammad Yunus** formed **11 reform commissions** to overhaul Bangladesh's institutions.

**Education consultancy regulation was not included in any commission's mandate.**

What was included:
- Police reform
- Election commission reform
- Anti-corruption reform
- Constitutional reform
- Judicial reform
- Media reform

**Why education consultancy was omitted:**
1. The uprising was about political freedom and institutional accountability — not consumer protection for study-abroad students
2. The reform commissions were heavily focused on political institutions, not economic regulation
3. No organised lobby pushed for education consultancy oversight
4. The interim government itself faces a massive governance backlog; education consultancy is not a priority crisis

**However**, the post-August environment does create **new enforcement opportunities**:
- The BFIU has been aggressive about asset recovery under the new government
- NBR is under pressure to raise the tax-to-GDP ratio (IMF conditionality)
- The ACC has launched new corruption investigations across sectors
- CID has been more active in financial fraud cases (BSB Global Network, 2024–2025)

> The window for enforcement is currently **wider than it has been in 15 years** — not because a new law was passed, but because the political protection networks that shielded fraudsters have temporarily weakened.

---

## Part 5: Why This Will Probably Continue

Unless a dedicated law is passed, the structural incentives remain unchanged:

1. **The informal economy will not self-regulate** — it has never done so in any sector in Bangladesh
2. **Post-Hasina political networks are reassembling** — new patronage relationships are forming; new consultancies will be protected by new political sponsors
3. **Foreign universities will not stop paying commissions** — the global education industry depends on agents in source countries; Korean, Australian, and Canadian institutions have their own incentives to look the other way
4. **Hundi demand is structural** — as long as the formal forex system is bureaucratic and expensive, hundi offers a better rate; students will continue using it
5. **DNCRP is reactive, not proactive** — without complaints, it does not act; with the complaint system under-publicised, most victims never file
6. **Victim silence enables repeat fraud** — BSB Global Network defrauded students for **six years** (2018–2024) before arrests were made; the same operators could re-emerge under a new company name in weeks

## Part 6: What Would Actually Fix It

For context — and for potential PIL or advocacy use — here is what evidence-based reform would look like:

| Reform | Model | Impact |
|--------|-------|--------|
| Mandatory education agency licence (Ministry of Education) | Australia's CRICOS, UK's UKVI Agent Accreditation | Creates blacklist; enables prosecution of unlicensed operators |
| Mandatory BIN registration regardless of turnover (for named service categories) | India's GST for education services | Captures all agencies; enables NBR audit |
| Mandatory written contract + receipt requirement | EU Distance Selling Directive | Creates paper trail for every student complaint |
| University commission disclosure | Australia's ESOS Act | Exposes undeclared foreign income |
| Public register of accredited agencies | British Council Agent accreditation | Enables students to verify before paying |
| National education agent database linked to BFIU | FATF Recommendation 28 | Identifies hundi flows through agency accounts |
| Enhanced penalties for agency fraud (dedicated section in CRPA) | Canada's Immigration Consultants of Canada Regulatory Council | Specific deterrent, not just general fraud law |

---

## Summary: The Real Answer

The reason there is no law regulating Bangladesh education consultancies, despite billions in uncollected taxes and hundreds of thousands of defrauded students, comes down to **six interlocking factors**:

> 1. **Nobody owns it** — five ministries pass the buck in a circle  
> 2. **The political-business nexus** benefits from unregulation — from consultancy operators connected to political networks, to hundi operators who move the money  
> 3. **The informal economy is Bangladesh's political economy** — 6.6% tax-to-GDP ratio is not a failure, it is the system working as designed for the powerful  
> 4. **The victims cannot organise** — young, dispersed, socially silenced, often abroad  
> 5. **The enforcement infrastructure does not exist** — NBR, DNCRP, and CID together cannot cover thousands of informal businesses  
> 6. **The law was never written for this market** — legislation was written before the market existed at scale, and nobody has had the political will to update it  

**The $234 billion siphoned from Bangladesh under the previous government is the macro version of the same dynamic.** The education consultancy black hole is simply one industry-level instance of a national-scale pattern: where regulation is absent, enforcement is weak, and the politically connected take their share before anyone can stop them.
