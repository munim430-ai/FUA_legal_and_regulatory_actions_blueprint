## 1. Current Status of Applicable Laws (2026)

> **Critical note:** Bangladesh's cyber law landscape changed three times in three years (2018→2023→2025). Always cite the current instrument. As of June 2026, the governing cyber law is the **Cyber Safety Ordinance, 2025**.

### 1.1 Consumer Rights Protection Act, 2009 (CRPA)

| Field | Status |
|-------|--------|
| **Current Status** | ✅ In Force |
| **Enforcing Body** | Directorate of National Consumer Rights Protection (DNCRP) |
| **Hotline** | **16121** (24/7) |
| **Portal** | www.dncrp.com |
| **Relevant Since** | Continuously enforced; no replacement as of 2026 |

The CRPA 2009 is the primary consumer protection statute. It covers deficient services, false advertising, refusal to refund, and unfair trade practices. Education consultancies that charge fees for services they do not deliver are directly subject to this Act.

**Key sections:**
- **S.2(20)** — "Service" includes any service rendered for consideration, explicitly encompassing education advisory, consultancy, and visa assistance
- **S.44** — Prohibits false/misleading advertising or claims about goods or services
- **S.45** — Prohibits sale of services at prices exceeding the displayed/advertised price
- **S.52** — Penalty for unfair trade practice: fine up to **BDT 2,00,000** and/or imprisonment up to **3 years**
- **S.76** — Complaint mechanism: any aggrieved person may file a complaint with DNCRP

---

### 1.2 Cyber Safety Ordinance, 2025 (CSO 2025) — **Current Law**

| Field | Status |
|-------|--------|
| **Previous Law** | Digital Security Act, 2018 (DSA) |
| **Intermediate Law** | Cyber Security Act, 2023 (CSA) — **Repealed** |
| **Current Law** | **Cyber Safety Ordinance, 2025** — effective **21 May 2025** |

**Legislative history in brief:**
- **DSA 2018** → broadly criminalised online "false information"
- **CSA 2023** (September 2023) → replaced DSA; retained most provisions including S.25 (false/offensive information) and S.26 (removing/altering digital content)
- **CSO 2025** (May 21, 2025) → replaced CSA 2023. **Sections 25 and 26 (false information, offensive content) were removed**. Any ongoing cases under those sections were annulled.

**What remains under CSO 2025 that applies to this case:**
- Online **identity fraud** and impersonation
- **Cyber terrorism** and data theft
- **Computer fraud and financial crime** using digital means
- Online **blackmail** and sexual harassment
- AI-generated content used for fraud

> **Implication:** False advertising claims on Facebook by Hangeul/WeCare can **no longer** be prosecuted under S.25/26 of the old CSA. However, they may still be actionable under the **Consumer Rights Protection Act 2009 S.44** (false advertising), **Penal Code S.420** (cheating by inducing delivery of property via false representation), and **FERA 1947** (for payment collection aspects).

### 1.3 Money Laundering Prevention Act, 2012 (MLPA)

| Field | Status |
|-------|--------|
| **Current Status** | ✅ In Force |
| **Amendments** | 2015 amendment (expanded investigative powers; multiple agencies can investigate) |
| **Supporting Rules** | Money Laundering Prevention Rules, 2019 (MLPR) |
| **Enforcing Body** | Bangladesh Financial Intelligence Unit (BFIU), within Bangladesh Bank |

**Key provisions for this case:**
- **S.2(v)** — "Money Laundering" includes concealing or disguising the nature, source, or ownership of proceeds of any offence including fraud
- **S.4** — Primary offence: imprisonment **4–12 years** + fine up to **BDT 10,00,000** (or double the laundered property value)
- **S.4(2)** — For entities: fine up to **BDT 20,00,000** + cancellation of registration
- Hundi (informal remittance) is a **predicate offence** under MLPA — proceeds collected via hundi circuits are treated as laundered money
- S.2 definition of "money value transferor" covers informal hawala/hundi networks explicitly

---

### 1.4 Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1947 (FERA)

| Field | Status |
|-------|--------|
| **Current Status** | ✅ In Force (not replaced as of 2026) |
| **Last Amendment** | 2015 |
| **Supporting Guidelines** | GFET 2018 (Guidelines for Foreign Exchange Transactions, 2 volumes) |
| **Enforcing Body** | Bangladesh Bank — Financial Intelligence Department (FID) |

**Key provisions:**
- **S.3** — Prohibits dealing in foreign exchange except through authorized dealers
- **S.4** — Prohibits payments to/from foreign persons without Bangladesh Bank authorization
- **S.8** — Prohibits export of foreign currency outside formal banking channels
- **S.23** — Penalty: imprisonment **4–12 years** + fine up to **twice the transaction value or BDT 20,00,000**

> Collecting Korean university tuition or visa fees in Bangladesh taka and transferring value informally to Korea (or to Korean-side facilitators) via hundi is a direct violation of FERA S.3 and S.8, punishable under S.23.

### 1.5 VAT and Supplementary Duty Act, 2012

| Field | Status |
|-------|--------|
| **Current Status** | ✅ In Force |
| **BIN Threshold** | Mandatory if annual turnover exceeds **BDT 80,00,000** (BDT 8 million) |
| **Below Threshold** | Turnover Tax (TT) applies at 4% for businesses BDT 30 lakh – 80 lakh |
| **Enforcing Body** | National Board of Revenue (NBR) — VAT Intelligence Unit |

**Key provisions:**
- **S.4** — Mandatory registration (BIN) requirement for service providers above threshold
- **S.46** — Obligation to issue Mushak-6.3 invoice for every taxable supply
- **S.57** — Prohibition on collecting VAT without being registered
- **S.85** — Penalty for non-registration: fine **BDT 10,000** minimum + prosecution
- **S.86–88** — Penalties for VAT fraud: up to **BDT 10,00,000** fine + imprisonment up to **5 years**

> An education consultancy charging fees per student (e.g., BDT 50,000–3,00,000 per student × multiple students = likely above BDT 80 lakh threshold) must be VAT-registered and issue proper Mushak-6.3 invoices. No receipt = no invoice = VAT evasion.

| Field | Status |
|-------|--------|
| **Current Status** | ✅ In Force |

| **Penalty for non-registration** | Fine + personal liability of directors; striking off; operations injunction |

### 1.8 Private University Act, 2010

| Field | Status |
|-------|--------|
| **Current Status** | ✅ In Force |
| **Regulator** | University Grants Commission of Bangladesh (UGC) |
| **Relevant for** | Agencies claiming to arrange admission to private universities or overseas institutions |

Agencies representing foreign universities (Korean or otherwise) to recruit Bangladeshi students must operate within UGC oversight. Unauthorized recruitment of students for foreign universities without proper agency agreements is a regulatory violation under the UGC mandate. While no dedicated licensing statute for education consultancies currently exists, the Ministry of Education has issued administrative circulars requiring registration with the Association of Education Consultants Bangladesh (AECB) for accreditation.

---

### 1.9 Regulatory Gap: No Mandatory Licensing for Education Consultancies (2026)

> **Confirmed finding (2026):** There is **no mandatory government licensing system** for overseas education consultancy firms in Bangladesh. A representative of one major consultancy was quoted: *"No, we do not have any registration from the government, nor do we need any."*

This is both the biggest fraud enabler and the biggest enforcement gap. As of 2026, firms need only:
1. A **Trade License** from City Corporation/Pourashava (easily obtained for any business)
2. **BIN registration** with NBR if turnover exceeds BDT 8 million

No specialised education agency licence, no Ministry of Education approval, no UGC authorisation, no minimum qualification requirements.

**This gap does not immunise fraudulent agencies.** They are still fully subject to:
- Penal Code (fraud/cheating/forgery)
- Consumer Rights Protection Act (false advertising, refund denial)
- FERA 1947 + MLPA 2012 (hundi, money laundering)
- VAT Act (BIN non-registration)
- RJSC / Trade License law

## 2. Laws Being Broken — Violation-by-Violation Analysis

### 2.1 False Advertising and Misleading Service Claims

**Alleged conduct:** Advertising guaranteed Korean university admission, scholarship claims, unrealistic visa approval rates, unverified university partnerships on Facebook and WhatsApp.

| Law | Section | Violation | Penalty |
|-----|---------|-----------|---------|
| Consumer Rights Protection Act, 2009 | S.44 | False/misleading advertising | Up to BDT 2,00,000 + 3 years imprisonment |
| Penal Code, 1860 | S.415–420 | Cheating by false representation to induce payment | Up to 7 years + fine |
| Cyber Safety Ordinance, 2025 | Chapter VI | Computer fraud using digital means | Varies |

> **Note:** S.25/26 (false information on internet) no longer applies after CSO 2025. The CRPA S.44 and Penal Code S.420 carry the primary load for false advertising now.

---

### 2.2 Collecting Fees Without Delivering Services / Refusal to Refund

**Alleged conduct:** Collecting BDT 50,000–3,00,000+ per student for visa processing and university application, then failing to deliver results and refusing refunds.

| Law | Section | Violation | Penalty |
|-----|---------|-----------|---------|
| Consumer Rights Protection Act, 2009 | S.45, S.52 | Deficient service, unfair trade practice | Fine up to BDT 2,00,000 + 3 years |
| Penal Code, 1860 | S.406 | Criminal breach of trust (money received for specific purpose misappropriated) | Up to 3 years + fine |
| Penal Code, 1860 | S.420 | Cheating — dishonestly inducing delivery of property | Up to 7 years + fine |

> **Precedent (2024–2025):** The BSB Global Network fraud case resulted in 141+ victims filing cases at Gulshan Police Station and CID filing a money laundering case after operators collected fees for Canadian student visas and refused refunds. This exact pattern mirrors Hangeul/WeCare operations.

---

### 2.3 Forged or Fabricated University Admission Documents

**Alleged conduct:** Providing fake admission letters, enrollment confirmations, or university affiliation certificates to students and the Korean embassy.

| Law | Section | Violation | Penalty |
|-----|---------|-----------|---------|
| Penal Code, 1860 | S.463–S.468 | Forgery of documents | Up to 7 years + fine |
| Penal Code, 1860 | S.471 | Using forged document as genuine | Same as forgery |
| Penal Code, 1860 | S.420 | Using forged documents to cheat | Up to 7 years + fine |

> Using forged Korean university documents to secure student visas is also a **visa fraud** under Korean immigration law, creating potential cross-border liability.

---

### 2.4 VAT / BIN Non-Registration and No Invoices

**Alleged conduct:** Collecting service fees from multiple students without issuing proper tax invoices (Mushak-6.3), without registering for BIN with NBR.

| Law | Section | Violation | Penalty |
|-----|---------|-----------|---------|
| VAT and SD Act, 2012 | S.4, S.85 | Failure to register for BIN when turnover exceeds threshold | Fine BDT 10,000 + prosecution |
| VAT and SD Act, 2012 | S.46, S.86 | Failure to issue Mushak-6.3 VAT invoice | Fine up to BDT 10,00,000 + 5 years imprisonment |
| VAT and SD Act, 2012 | S.57 | Collecting fees without VAT registration (de facto VAT fraud) | As above |

> **Practical threshold:** If a consultancy charges BDT 1,00,000 per student and handles 80+ students per year, it crosses the BDT 80 lakh mandatory registration threshold. Even below this, Turnover Tax registration is still required above BDT 30 lakh.

---

### 2.5 Hundi / Illegal Remittance to Korea

**Alleged conduct:** Receiving student tuition/visa fees in Bangladesh taka, then transferring value informally to Korean universities, Korean agents, or Korean bank accounts via hundi networks (typically using agents in Korea receiving taka-equivalent in won).

| Law | Section | Violation | Penalty |
|-----|---------|-----------|---------|
| FERA, 1947 | S.3, S.4, S.8 | Dealing in foreign exchange outside authorized banking channels | 4–12 years imprisonment + fine up to 2× transaction value |
| MLPA, 2012 | S.4 | Money laundering — concealing/transferring proceeds via informal channels | 4–12 years + fine up to BDT 10,00,000 |

> Bangladesh Bank has issued explicit warnings (2022–2024) about digital hundi targeting the study-abroad market. Consultancies routing student money outside formal banking violate both FERA and MLPA simultaneously.

## 3. Can a 2025 Student Recover Their Money?

### 3.1 Short Answer

**Yes — partial or full recovery is legally possible, but difficult and time-consuming.** The best realistic outcomes are through DNCRP (fastest), civil money suit (medium-term), or criminal prosecution with compensation order (slowest, but possible).

---

### 3.2 Route A — DNCRP Complaint (Fastest: 30–90 days)

**Who can file:** The student directly, or an authorised representative on their behalf.

**How:**
1. Call **16121** (24/7 hotline) to register initial complaint
2. File written complaint at: TCB Building, 9th Floor, 1 Karwan Bazar, Dhaka (or nearest district DNCRP office)
3. Online: www.dncrp.com → "File Complaint"
4. Attach: fee receipts, WhatsApp screenshots, service agreement (if any), bank transfer records, passport/visa records

**What DNCRP can order:**
- **Full or partial refund** from the service provider
- Fine of up to **25% of the service price** paid to the complainant as compensation
- Fine on the agency up to BDT 2,00,000
- Criminal referral to police if fraud is established

**Limitation:** DNCRP is a quasi-judicial administrative body. If the agency ignores the order, enforcement requires further court action. However, DNCRP actively publishes defaulting businesses and can refer cases to the ACC or police.

**Precedent:** In numerous 2023–2025 consumer fraud cases involving travel and education agencies, DNCRP has ordered refunds within 45–60 days of complaint filing.

---

### 3.3 Route B — Civil Money Suit (Medium: 1–3 years)

Under the **Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC)**, a student can file a civil money suit in the appropriate Court of Civil Judge or District Judge (depending on claim amount):

- **Court:** Civil Courts at the jurisdiction where the agency office is located
- **Claim:** Recovery of fees paid + damages for loss suffered (visa denial costs, travel costs, missed academic year)
- **Limitation period:** **3 years** from the date the cause of action arose (i.e., 3 years from when service was denied/money withheld) under the Limitation Act, 1908
- **Cost:** Court fee (ad valorem on claim amount) + lawyer fees
- **Timeline:** 1–3 years (civil courts are backlogged, but money suits against clear-cut fraud cases tend to move faster)

**Key advantage:** A civil decree can be enforced by attachment of the agency's bank accounts, property, and assets.

---

### 3.4 Route C — Criminal Complaint with Compensation (Slowest: 2–5 years, but possible)

Filing a criminal case under **S.420** (cheating) or **S.406** (criminal breach of trust) with the local police station:

1. File a **First Information Report (FIR)** at the police station where the crime occurred (where the agency office is, or where payment was made)
2. Police investigate and file **charge sheet**
3. Upon conviction, the court can order **compensation** to the victim under **S.545 of CrPC**

> **Critical finding from BSB Global Network case (2024–2025):** Even with criminal cases filed, 141+ victims have not yet recovered funds. Criminal proceedings primarily punish offenders; money recovery requires a **separate civil execution** step.

**CID Cyber Crime Unit (faster for digital fraud):**
If the fraud was transacted via Facebook, WhatsApp, or online banking, CID Cyber Crime Unit has concurrent jurisdiction and faster processing than regular police.

---

### 3.5 Route D — Korea-Side Remedies (for students already in Korea)

A student who went to Korea in 2025 and discovers fraud after arrival has additional options:

**Korean Consumer Agency (한국소비자원):**
- File complaint at: www.kca.go.kr (Korean Consumer Agency)
- Jurisdiction: If any service contract element was performed in Korea (e.g., Korean university received payment, Korean language school operated in Korea)
- The Consumer Dispute Mediation Committee can mediate for refunds

**Korean Anti-Corruption and Civil Rights Commission (ACRC):**
- File civil complaints about institutional misconduct at: www.acrc.go.kr
- Relevant if Korean universities or Korean institutions were complicit in the fraud

**Korean Ministry of Justice / Immigration:**
- Report visa fraud to Korean Immigration: **1345** (Immigration Contact Center)
- If a student's visa was obtained via forged documents, they may be categorised as a **victim** rather than a perpetrator if they can demonstrate they were defrauded
- Korean Immigration Service (HiKorea portal) allows report of fraudulent agents

**Limitation:** Korean authorities have no jurisdiction over Bangladeshi consultancies. However, a formal Korean complaint creates documentary evidence for Bangladesh proceedings and may trigger a joint investigation if the Korean university was also defrauded.

---

### 3.6 What the BSB Global Network Case Teaches Us (2024–2025)

The BSB Global Network case is the closest precedent to this situation:
- **Scheme:** Promised Canadian student visas/admissions, collected BDT 1.3–1.7 million per victim
- **Victims:** 448 formal complainants; estimated 1,000+ actual victims; fraud estimated at BDT 200 crore+
- **Legal response:** CID money laundering case filed; chairman arrested; travel ban issued
- **Recovery status as of 2025:** **No funds recovered yet** for victims
- **Lesson:** Criminal prosecution proceeds faster than victim compensation. Students must simultaneously pursue **DNCRP complaint** (fastest route for refund) AND **CID criminal complaint** (for accountability), not one or the other.

## 4. Third-Party Complaint Rights

### 4.1 Bangladesh — Can I, as a Third Party, File?

**Yes — with important nuances by authority:**

#### DNCRP (Consumer Rights Protection Act, 2009)
- **S.76(1):** Any person who "can generally be a consumer" may file a complaint
- The provision has been interpreted broadly by DNCRP to allow complaints by:
  - NGOs acting on behalf of victims
  - Journalists with documented evidence
  - Concerned individuals with evidence of fraud
- **Practical approach:** File as an "informant/witness" with evidence; DNCRP will verify by contacting victims
- **Best practice:** Attach victim testimonies (with their written consent) along with OSINT documentation

#### Bangladesh Police / CID (Penal Code, CrPC)
- **Any person** can file a complaint with police under S.154/S.155 of CrPC even if not a direct victim
- Third-party complaints trigger a police **General Diary (GD)** which escalates to FIR upon preliminary verification
- **S.200 CrPC:** Any magistrate can take cognizance of a complaint filed by any person (not just the victim)

#### BFIU / Bangladesh Bank (MLPA 2012)
- **Any reporting organisation OR any person** can file a Suspicious Transaction Report (STR) via:
  - **goAML portal:** Bangladesh Bank's official STR submission system
  - Direct letter to BFIU Head Office, Bangladesh Bank, Motijheel C/A, Dhaka
- Third-party STRs trigger BFIU investigation without requiring victim cooperation

#### NBR VAT Intelligence (VAT Act 2012)
- **Any person** can report suspected VAT evasion to NBR's Customs, Excise & VAT Commissionerate
- Informants are protected under NBR regulations; a portion of recovered tax may be awarded to the informant

#### Public Interest Litigation (PIL) — High Court Division
- **Any citizen** can file a PIL before the High Court Division (writ petition) against:
  - Government bodies failing to regulate education consultancies
  - Agencies operating in violation of consumer protection laws
  - Demanding enforcement of existing regulations
- **No direct victim status required** for PIL; petitioner must show public interest

#### Anti-Corruption Commission (ACC)
- If fraud involves public officials (e.g., BMET/Ministry of Education staff facilitating unlicensed agencies), third-party complaint to ACC at **acc.org.bd** or hotline **106**

---

### 4.2 Protections for Third-Party Complainants

| Authority | Protection |
|-----------|------------|
| DNCRP | Identity can be kept confidential; complainant is not required to appear at every hearing |
| Police/CID | Complaint can be submitted anonymously (GD); formal FIR requires identity but is protected |
| BFIU/goAML | STR submitters are legally protected from retaliation under MLPA S.22; identity protected |
| NBR | Informant identity protected under NBR rules |
| ACC | Whistleblower protection under ACC Act; identity protected |

---

### 4.3 Journalists and NGOs — Special Standing

Bangladesh courts have recognized **NGOs and journalists** as having standing to file complaints on behalf of public interest. Key precedents:

- Under PIL jurisprudence, Bangladeshi courts have accepted writ petitions by consumer rights organizations
- Consumer associations registered under the Societies Registration Act 1860 have direct standing before DNCRP
- Media investigations that document fraud have triggered suo motu (on its own initiative) regulatory action by DNCRP and NBR

## 5. Filing Guide — Authority by Authority

### 5.1 DNCRP — Consumer Complaint (Recommended First Step)

**For:** Refund, compensation, public shaming, administrative penalty  
**Timeline:** 30–90 days  
**Cost:** Free (no court fee)

```
Step 1: Call 16121 (24/7) → state complaint in 2–3 sentences → get complaint reference number
Step 2: Submit written complaint to:
        National Consumer Complaint Centre (NCCC)
        TCB Building, 9th Floor, 1 Karwan Bazar, Dhaka-1215
        Email: nccc@dncrp.gov.bd | Web: www.dncrp.com
Step 3: Attach evidence:
        - Fee receipts / bank transfer screenshots
        - WhatsApp/Facebook screenshots of false claims
        - Any written agreement with the agency
        - Names/contact details of victims (with their consent if possible)
Step 4: DNCRP will issue summons to the agency; hearing within 21 days
Step 5: DNCRP can order refund + fine within 45–60 days
```

**As a third party:** File as "informant" with victim evidence and consent. DNCRP will verify directly.

---

|--------|----------------------|----------|----------------|
| DNCRP complaint (with receipts) | **HIGH** (70–80%) | 30–90 days | Partial-Full |
| NBR VAT evasion report | **HIGH** (if BIN check shows no registration) | 30–60 days | No (penalty on agency) |
| BFIU STR (hundi) | **MEDIUM** (50–60%) | 30–180 days | Possible asset freeze |
| CID criminal complaint (S.420) | **MEDIUM** (40–60%) | 6–18 months | Indirect only |
| Civil money suit (CPC) | **MEDIUM-HIGH** (60–70% with receipts) | 1–3 years | Yes (decree + execution) |
| Korean Embassy visa fraud report | **MEDIUM** | 30–90 days | No (stops future fraud) |
| PIL (High Court) | **LOW-MEDIUM** (30–40%) | 6–24 months | No (systemic change) |

### 7.2 Key Risk Factors

1. **Lack of formal agreement:** Most unlicensed consultancies deliberately avoid written contracts. WhatsApp evidence + bank transfer records substitute effectively.

2. **Asset concealment:** Fraudulent agencies often have minimal registered assets. BFIU freeze orders are the most effective countermeasure.

3. **Agency closure/relocation:** Fraudsters shut down and reopen under new names. This is why **RJSC/trade license complaints** simultaneously filed create a paper trail.

4. **No mandatory licensing = minimal regulatory deterrent:** Until Bangladesh enacts mandatory education consultancy licensing, enforcement relies entirely on post-hoc fraud prosecution. However, all the laws above are fully applicable.

5. **Korean jurisdiction limit:** Bangladesh courts cannot compel Korean entities. If a Korean university was complicit, the student's only Korean-side recourse is the Korea Consumer Agency or filing with Korean Immigration.

### 7.3 Third-Party Complainant (You) — Most Effective Actions

As a third party (not a direct victim), your highest-impact actions in order of effect:

1. **File BFIU STR** → can freeze accounts without victim cooperation, anonymously
2. **File NBR VAT evasion report** → does not require victim; agency tax records are sufficient
3. **File DNCRP complaint** with victim evidence attached → with victim consent, you file on their behalf
4. **File police GD/FIR** with CID → with victim consent and documented evidence
5. **Contact Korean Embassy Dhaka** with fake admission document evidence → triggers Korean-side scrutiny
6. **Approach media** → documented OSINT + victim stories have triggered DNCRP suo motu action in Bangladesh before
